AI Is Evolving — Are You?

When the smartest tool in the room is slowly making you a worse thinker.

Picture this. A friend uses ChatGPT to write a tricky email to her boss. She skims it, hits send, feels efficient. The reply comes back confused. She reads her own email — really reads it, for the first time — and realizes she doesn't quite agree with what she sent under her own name.

This is the quiet pattern of modern AI use. We ask. We accept. We send. We code with copilots and ship what they suggest. We follow GPS without looking up. Each moment feels like a small win. Stack enough of them together, and something else starts happening: the muscles we used to flex — judging, verifying, paying attention — get a little weaker each time we skip them.

01

The conversation we're not having

Most of what you hear about AI right now focuses on one side of the equation: making the AI smarter. Bigger models, better benchmarks, more autonomous agents, AI that can improve itself.

We think this is the wrong place to put all our attention. In our recent position paper, we argue that human evolution should be treated as a goal of AI progress, not an afterthought. As AI takes on more of our work, the skills we need to use it well don't appear automatically. They have to be deliberately practiced. And the same automation that makes us more productive often quietly removes our chances to practice them.

Better humans make better AI. Worse humans make worse AI. The loop runs in both directions.

There's a feedback loop here that almost nobody talks about. When you silently accept a polished-but-wrong AI answer, that's not just a personal cost. That signal — "human approved" — flows back into how the next generation of AI is trained. Models learn that confident, polished, agreeable answers get rewarded. The result is sycophantic AI that tells you what sounds good, taught by humans who stopped checking.

The uncomfortable question: as AI gets dramatically more capable, are we getting better at using it? Or are we quietly becoming worse?

02

Four phases of using AI — and where things quietly break

To make this concrete, we mapped four phases of how people actually use AI, based on how much work you hand over. Each phase brings a new kind of empowerment, a new kind of risk, and asks you to practice a different skill. The same pattern repeats across phases: you gain capability, you lose practice, and the underlying ability fades unless you protect it.

Phase 1 · Tool

Pocket Expert

You ask, it answers.

Scenario
Definitions, summaries, "explain it like I'm five," "what's the best way to…" — the AI gives you knowledge that used to take a search session, a textbook chapter, or a phone call to a colleague.
What breaks
Your reasoning gets rusty. It's so easy to get an answer that you stop forming your own first. You drift toward passive acceptance.
Practice
Critical thinking. Before you ask, predict the answer yourself. Then compare. The AI becomes feedback on your reasoning instead of a substitute for it.
AI's job
Show uncertainty. Surface the evidence it's working from. Ask what you already think before answering.
+18%  Doctors who trusted AI heavily accepted that many more misdiagnoses than doctors who trusted it less. Srinivasan et al., 2025
Phase 2 · Assistant

Personal Assistant

You delegate, it produces.

Scenario
Draft emails, code snippets, slide decks, marketing copy, first-pass analyses. AI moves from answering questions to producing finished-looking work you check and ship.
What breaks
Your verification goes shallow. Polished output looks right. Polish makes things feel right. So you check less, and you start trusting surface signals — length, formatting, confident tone — instead of the substance.
Practice
Evaluative expertise. Decide what tests an output must pass before you generate it. Ask the AI for two or three different versions — if they disagree, that's a signal to look harder.
AI's job
Stop hiding its work behind a polished final draft. Show its assumptions. Point at where it's most likely to be wrong. Surface disagreements across attempts.
40%  of code written with GitHub Copilot in one study had security vulnerabilities — and developers rated their Copilot work as more secure than their solo work. Perry et al., 2023; Pearce et al., 2022
Phase 3 · Executor

Autopilot

You set a goal, it runs a whole workflow.

Scenario
Coding agents that write, test, and revise entire features. Analytics agents that query databases, transform data, and report. Driving systems that handle most of the trip. You set the destination; the system does the doing.
What breaks
Your oversight breaks down. Watching is harder than driving. You miss the intermediate cues you'd notice if you were operating the system yourself, and you're slower to intervene when things drift off-course.
Practice
Metacognitive monitoring. Decide in advance where you'll check in. Identify the steps in the workflow that matter most and concentrate your attention there, instead of skimming everything thinly.
AI's job
Don't run silently to the finish. Surface progress. Pause at predefined checkpoints. Flag when confidence is low or actions cross a risk threshold.
97.4%  of users in one experiment with browser agents failed to interrupt the agent when it was about to accept a malicious privacy policy on their behalf. They were watching. They weren't really seeing. Chen et al., 2025
Phase 4 · Organization (near future)

Orchestrator

You set direction; AI coordinates a system of agents.

Scenario
One AI breaks a goal into pieces, hands them to other AIs, manages their interactions, evaluates the results. This isn't widespread yet, but managed-agent architectures in research and software are starting to show it.
What breaks
Governance becomes opaque. You see the final output. You don't see how the work was divided, who did what, where things were rerouted, or why pieces were redone. If something is off, you can't tell whether one component failed or the whole system was pointed at the wrong target.
Practice
Systems thinking. Define rules before you deploy: what's forbidden, where the risk thresholds are, what gets escalated. Then audit samples of trajectories — you can't watch every step, but you can statistically check that the system is still behaving.
AI's job
Summarize coordination decisions in plain language. Preserve audit trails that connect what was done back to the original goal. Flag high-risk branches so humans can dig in where it matters.
Phase 4 is forward-looking. The risk is structural: outcome-only feedback can't tell you whether a failure came from one weak agent or from how the work was orchestrated — so the coordination layer stays under-improved.
The pattern that repeats Each phase gives you more leverage and quietly removes a chance to practice the skill that makes that leverage safe. The skill doesn't fall off a cliff. It thins out. Then one day you notice you're trusting the answer because it looks confident, not because you checked.

A quick way to locate yourself if you can't find your exact role: what's the largest unit of work you currently hand to AI without checking each step? A sentence? A document? A whole task? A project with subtasks farmed out across agents? That's your phase.

Different phases ask for different skills. You don't have to master all four right now. You do need to know which one your work has already reached — because that's the skill that's quietly being practiced less, every time you hand off another piece.

04

Three habits to start practicing this week

You don't have to wait for AI policy reform to start evolving. Three habits, small enough to start today, that map directly onto the skills above:

01

Pre-think

Before you ask AI a question, take thirty seconds to write down what you think the answer is, or what you're uncertain about. Then ask. Then compare. This one habit alone changes your relationship with AI from "answer machine" to "thinking partner."

02

Critique

Read every AI output once with the assumption that it's wrong somewhere. Where would the mistake be hiding? Polish is the enemy of careful reading — train yourself to look past it. The flaw is usually in the spot that sounds the most confident.

03

Disconnect

Once a week, do a task you usually delegate to AI — entirely without it. Write the email. Debug the function. Plan the route. Not because AI is bad, but because this is how you check whether the underlying skill is still there.

Why this works These aren't anti-AI practices. They're what makes pro-AI use sustainable. Each one carves out a small space where you do the work AI would otherwise absorb — just enough that the underlying capability stays alive and informs your judgment when AI is wrong, unavailable, or operating outside what it was built for.
05

The future we're betting on

There's a version of the future where AI gets dramatically more capable and humans get dramatically less so — where we drift into a world we no longer understand and can't course-correct. It's not a science-fiction scenario. It's the natural endpoint of a feedback loop we're already running, in which polish gets rewarded over correctness and the people who could push back are slowly losing the practice that would let them.

There's another version. In it, AI runs fast and humans see far. AI handles volume and humans handle values. AI does the work and humans direct it — not because that's how things have always been, but because both sides have grown into roles that fit together. Academia teaches phase-specific skills. Industry builds review pipelines, checkpoints, and audit trails into how agents are deployed. Society sets the rules so that what counts as "human oversight" actually matches what humans can realistically see.

That future doesn't happen by accident. It depends on humans who keep growing, not just the tools they build.

The good news: you're closer to it than you think. It starts the next time you open a chat window — and pause for thirty seconds before you ask.

AI runs fast. Humans see far. The future depends on both.
